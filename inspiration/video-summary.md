# Steven Pinker on writing, and on what LLMs write like

This summarizes a recorded interview with Steven Pinker, filmed in his
office at Harvard. Pinker studies language and the mind, has written nine
books for general readers, and wrote a style manual, *The Sense of
Style*. The interviewer asks him first how to write well, then what large
language models do to prose.

## Bad writing is incompetence, not a scam

Ask why academic and corporate writing is unreadable, Pinker says, and
most people reach for a villain: the scholar with nothing to say who
buries a thin idea in gobbledygook so it looks deep. He doesn't buy it.
He knows too many brilliant people who write badly, and he invokes
Hanlon's razor — don't blame malice for what dullness explains.

The culprit is what economists call the curse of knowledge: you cannot
subtract from your own head the thing you already know. His example: a
molecular biologist at TED opened with the jargon he'd use on his lab's colleagues,
never said what problem he was attacking or why it mattered, and lost
several hundred people in about four seconds. Everyone saw it but him.

Writing to an audience you cannot see makes it worse. Two people talking
share a reason for standing there, so "this" and "that thing we
discussed" carry weight. A reader picks your book off a shelf in another
country, years later; you may be dead. Everything has to be on the page.
Speech also hands you the furrowed brow and the fidget; the reader's
confusion never reaches you.

His own defense is partial and he says so. Imagining not knowing fails by
definition, so he hands drafts to people. His mother read every one while
she was alive — not as a stand-in for a dull reader, he insists; she was
sharp and widely read, just not a psycholinguist.

## Give the reader something to see

The ideas language carries are usually sensory, he says — visual, bodily,
heard — not a string of vowels and consonants. So the writer's job is to
keep an image forming. Say bunny rabbit, not stimulus; nobody has ever
pictured a paradigm. Abstractions do earn their keep
inside a field — a biologist can't rebuild chemistry every time he needs
"potentiation" — but the damage comes when the word follows him out of
the building and he stops noticing it is specialized.

The same logic drives his rule that generalizations and examples are
useless apart. A generalization sweeps the particulars away, and abstract
words are too loose to pin down which meaning you want. He demonstrates
rather than asserts: the claim that a familiar word needn't mean the sum
of its parts sits inert until you hear that a bathroom holds no bath and
breakfast breaks no fast.

## Sound, and the gift of a word limit

Pinker mumbles his prose aloud. If he can't say a sentence smoothly, the
reader won't run it smoothly in their head either. He listens for the
beat — language has rhythm, and fighting it slows a reader on the page as
it would in the mouth. He swaps synonyms to break up hissing runs of *s*,
and likes a little alliteration, faint enough not to look arranged.

He is an enthusiast for hard limits. Shakespeare's brevity is the soul of
wit works because "soul" is one syllable doing the job of "essential
component," and because the line obeys itself. When a magazine gives him
800 words and an editor lops off anything past 883 without asking which
three to lose, the squeeze improves the piece: it burns off woolly idiom
and pushes him toward concrete words.

## Why old prose reads richer, and what that says about AI

Rockefeller carved on stone at Rockefeller Center, Edison on electricity
and women's labor, Darwin — none of them professional writers, all vivid.
Pinker gives three reasons. They were raised on the classics, with good
models to copy. Prose was how you presented yourself, so you groomed it
the way you'd check a mirror. And they had few ready-made abstractions to
grab, so to say aggression they reached for an image — a hawk's spirit in
the flesh. He sets this against a long slide he calls informalization:
hats and gloves gone, first names everywhere, crafted phrasing now read
as putting on airs.

LLM prose, he says, is the far end of the canned-phrase habit. It is
sound — plain sentences, orderly paragraphs, an opener and a closer — and
that alone beats most lawyers and bureaucrats. But it is generic enough
that you can spot it. He guesses at two causes: human feedback hammering
it into the shape of a five-paragraph essay, and an averaging effect like
the one in faces, where morphing a yearbook's hundreds of students yields
someone better-looking than any one of them. Averaged prose, he suspects,
comes out clear but not beautiful.

Asked whether he'd rewrite his books, Pinker says probably. Trained on
rules, logic, and Chomskyan structure, he would now grant more to what
pattern-extraction from huge piles of text can do — while stopping short
of saying a mind works that way. A child would need 30,000 years of
listening to match the input, and children learn inside a world they can
point at.
